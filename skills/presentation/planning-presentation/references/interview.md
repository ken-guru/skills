# Interview

## What the Brief needs

| Topic | The question behind it |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who will be in the room, what do they already know, and what do they care about? |
| Goal | What should they think, feel, or do afterwards? |
| Occasion | What event, when, where, in person or online, and who else is speaking? |
| Length | How many minutes, and how much of it for questions? |
| Language | Which language, and any terms that must stay as they are? |
| Constraints | Anything that must or must not be said, branding, confidentiality, accessibility needs |
| Look | Light (Editorial) or dark (Editorial Inverse), or the organisation's colours, fonts, and logo |
| Editorial preferences | Tone, styles to prefer, styles to avoid |
| Sources | What the person already has, and how much research the talk needs |

## Rounds

Ask everything you can answer now in one round. A question that depends on an answer you have not heard yet belongs to a later round.

Format each question so "yes" accepts your recommendation:

```
❓ **Q1 - Audience**: Who is this for? I'm assuming engineering managers who know the codebase but not the new tool.

➡️ Engineering managers, familiar with the codebase, new to the tool.
```

## Challenging premises

Listen for assumptions the talk rests on, and test them before building on them:

- "Everyone agrees this is a problem." Who might not, and what would convince them?
- "The data shows…" Which data, and does the person have it?
- "Managers decide this." Is that the audience that can act on the goal?

When a premise looks shaky, say why in one sentence and ask how to proceed. When research later contradicts a premise, bring it back to the person and update the Brief.

## When to stop

Stop when you could explain the talk's audience, goal, and key constraint to someone else without guessing. Then write the Brief and ask for confirmation.
